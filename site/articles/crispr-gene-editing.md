---
title: CRISPR Is a Repurposed Bacterial Immune System
category: Science
summary: Before it was a gene-editing tool, CRISPR was how bacteria remember viruses. The tool works because the immune system already worked.
---

CRISPR gets described as a technology humans invented, and that framing gets the story backward. CRISPR is billions of years old. Humans didn't invent it — they found it, in bacteria, doing a job that has nothing to do with editing genomes and everything to do with bacteria trying not to die.

Bacteria get infected by viruses called bacteriophages, constantly, and losing that fight means the cell dies. Some bacteria evolved a defense that works like a mugshot book. When a bacterium survives a phage infection, it cuts out a small piece of the invading virus's DNA and files it away in its own genome, in a region called CRISPR — Clustered Regularly Interspaced Short Palindromic Repeats. That name describes the physical structure: short repeated DNA sequences with unique "spacer" sequences (the captured viral snippets) sitting between them, like index cards filed between dividers.

The filing is only half the system. The other half is recognition. The bacterium builds RNA copies of those stored viral snippets and hands them to a protein — in the most widely used version, a protein called Cas9 — which acts like a guided pair of scissors. The RNA guide leads Cas9 along the cell's DNA, checking for a sequence match. If that same virus infects again, its DNA matches one of the filed mugshots, Cas9 locks on, and cuts the viral DNA to shreds before it can take over the cell. It's an adaptive immune system, conceptually not that different from how human immune cells remember a pathogen after first exposure — except it operates directly on DNA sequence, and bacteria had it first by a wide margin.

The insight that turned this into a laboratory tool, credited to Jennifer Doudna and Emmanuelle Charpentier (Nobel Prize, 2020), was noticing that the "search and cut" mechanism doesn't actually care whether the target sequence came from a virus. Cas9 finds and cuts whatever sequence its guide RNA tells it to look for. Swap the natural viral-derived guide RNA for a synthetic one matching any sequence you choose — a gene tied to a disease, a specific mutation — and Cas9 will find that sequence in a genome and cut it there, wherever it happens to sit among three billion base pairs.

A cut alone isn't the edit. Once Cas9 slices the DNA, the cell's own repair machinery takes over, and this is the second piece of borrowed biology. Cells have always needed to fix double-strand breaks — from radiation, from chemical damage, from ordinary wear — and they have two main ways of doing it. One, called non-homologous end joining, just glues the cut ends back together, often imperfectly, which tends to disable whatever gene was cut — useful for knocking a gene out. The other, homology-directed repair, uses a matching DNA template to patch the break precisely, and if scientists supply their own template alongside Cas9, the cell will use it, inserting a new sequence at the cut site rather than just closing the wound. CRISPR editing is really three repurposed systems stacked together: bacterial memory, bacterial targeting, and the cell's own repair machinery, none of which evolved for this purpose.

That layered borrowing is why CRISPR spread through labs so fast after 2012, compared to earlier gene-editing tools like zinc finger nucleases, which required engineering a new custom protein for every new DNA target — slow and expensive. CRISPR only requires designing a new guide RNA, a short sequence that's cheap and fast to synthesize, while the cutting protein stays the same. The hard, expensive part of gene editing had already been solved — by bacteria, for their own reasons, a very long time before anyone was trying to cure anything.
